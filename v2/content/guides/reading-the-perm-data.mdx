DOL publishes figures about the PERM queue, and most of the confusion
around processing times comes from reading them as something they aren’t.
This guide walks through each number on PERM Tracker's [data overview](/tools),
which updates the day DOL changes its figures. Then it covers each of the data
pages and each of the [calculators](/calculators), in the order a case meets them.

## Start with your own case number

[PERM case status](/perm-case-status) takes one PERM case number and shows
what DOL holds against it. It gives the status in DOL's own words with a
plain-English reading beside it, how many undecided cases were filed ahead
of it, and the sponsoring employer's published record. The statuses come
from DOL's own case-status search, which PERM Tracker runs in batches
against every undecided case twice a day. So a case decided in the last few
hours shows at DOL first.

There are two limits to know before you type a number in. Current case numbers
look like `G-100-26125-868956`. The shorter 2022 and 2023 form
(`A-23043-00641`) is looked up too, but its middle block isn't a readable
filing date, so no queue position is drawn from one. And a status alert can only be set on a
case that can still change, because there's nothing to report on a case DOL
has already decided.

## The queue position

The headline figure on the [overview](/tools) is the filing month DOL is
deciding right now in analyst review, the queue nearly every case sits in.
Subtract your filing month from that month and you have your rough distance
in queue terms. The tape below it draws the same fact: cleared months
solid, waiting months empty, the frontier flagged.

Keep two cautions in mind. The position is a month, and DOL clears a month
over weeks, so it can't promise a date. Audited cases also
sit in a separate, slower queue, and DOL hasn't printed where it stands since October 31, 2026.

## The average, and why it disagrees with everyone

DOL also publishes an average number of days to a determination. On
August 24, 2026 it said 372, while public tools reported anywhere from 335 to
503 that same day\. The spread has mechanical causes (different populations, different
windows, different data ages), and the [methodology page](/methodology)
takes them apart one by one. When two numbers disagree, the first question
is what each one is measuring, and the second is when its data was
published.

## How much is still waiting, and how fast DOL is clearing it

DOL's quarterly disclosure files carry a decision date on every row and no
pending rows at all, so the size of its own backlog can't be derived from
them at any level of effort. [The backlog page](/perm-queue) is built from
a per-case scan instead: how many cases are still undecided in each filing
month, and which DOL queue each one is standing in.

[Decision activity](/perm-decision-activity) is the other half of the same
question. It counts the decisions DOL issued by day and by week. It also
shows the shape of its working week and what happened to the cases it decided.
The page reports what has already happened and forecasts nothing, because the
rate the queue moved last month isn't a promise about next month.

## RFI, audits and appeals

[The review-stage page](/perm-rfi-audit) covers the statuses DOL puts in
front of an applicant with no explanation attached: RFI issued, pending
audit response, supervised recruitment, reconsideration, BALCA. Each one
gets what it means, who has to act, the deadline the regulation sets, and
the section it comes from. A live count sits beside it, showing how many cases
are standing at each stage. It exists nowhere else, because the
disclosure files hold no pending cases.

The page publishes no audit rate and no probability that a given case draws
an RFI. A rate over a group isn't a forecast for a member of it. PERM
Tracker's own audit measurement currently reads zero, which is a hole in
the data rather than a finding. DOL's [processing
times](https://flag.dol.gov/processingtimes) page publishes a live Audit
Review queue, so the true figure plainly isn't zero.

## Which calculator answers which question

**Before filing.** The [prevailing wage queue](/tools/pwd-calculator) counts
how many wage requests sit ahead of the month yours was received, with no
model involved. The [deadline calculator](/tools/perm-deadline-calculator) is
the one exact tool in the set. Recruitment windows, the quiet period and the
ETA-9089 filing window are arithmetic in the regulations, so those dates are
computed rather than estimated.

**While waiting.** The [decision estimator](/tools/perm-timeline-calculator)
shows every model its data supports for your filing month, labelled, and lets
them disagree. If you want one email when DOL reaches your month, set a
[queue alert](/perm-processing-times). It fires once and nothing else
follows it.

**After certification.** The [I-140 calculator](/tools/i140-calculator) puts
USCIS's published processing time next to its own queue arithmetic, and the
gap between the two is the useful part. The
[priority date calculator](/tools/priority-date-calculator) shows where a
date stands against the visa bulletin and which way the cutoff has been
moving. It moves backwards more often than you might expect.

**The whole picture.** The [green card timeline](/tools/green-card-timeline)
draws every stage to scale, including the one nobody can number. That stage
is the wait for a visa number. It depends on the bulletin, so the timeline
shows it as an open question with no figure.

## The whole field: states, wages, sponsors

The disclosure files carry more than timing, and three pages cut the same
quarter three ways. The [state map](/perm-by-state) places every filing at
its worksite state. Volume and wages swing hard by state, but median days
barely move, because DOL works one national queue wherever the case was
filed. The [wages page](/perm-wages) gives median offered wages by
occupation. These are committed figures from federal filings rather than
survey estimates. And the [employers page](/perm-employers) ranks every
sponsor in the window, searchable, with each one's approval rate and median
days. It's useful for checking a sponsor's track record. It can't predict
your own case's pace, which only the filing month decides.

## Who filed it, and what gets denied

Two more cuts of the same files answer questions the aggregates above
can’t. The [law firms page](/perm-attorneys) ranks every firm filing PERM
cases. It's the only public benchmark an attorney has for their own
practice against the field. It also lets a beneficiary see whether the
firm on their case has done this before. The [denial rates
page](/perm-denial-risk) shows what gets denied: by offered wage,
by fiscal year, and by the three questions the ETA-9089 asks about layoffs,
ownership interest and full-time status. Denials are rare and concentrated,
and none of those rates is a probability for a single case.

## For caseloads

Every date the deadline calculator produces, the [tracker](/signup)
computes automatically per case, with alerts and calendar sync. The public
calculators and the tracker run the same regulatory arithmetic from one
shared engine, so the two can never disagree.
