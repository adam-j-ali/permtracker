DOL has published its proposal to rewrite the PERM rules. It went on the Federal Register's public inspection list on November 16, 2026, and the Register published it on November 17 (91 FR 73916). Here's what it would change, set beside the rule that applies today. Nothing changes for a case until a final rule takes effect. PERM Tracker's [policy changes page](/policy-changes) follows each step from here, from White House review to the Federal Register, comment deadline included.

## Where it stands

The proposal is open for comment, and its latest step is tracked live below.

<RuleStatus rin="1205-AC29" />

The proposal fills 107 pages of the Register, under docket ETA-2026-0133. Anyone can comment, with a comment deadline of January 19, 2027. DOL has to consider the comments before it can publish a final rule, and only a final rule changes 20 CFR 656.

Some things stay as they are. The I-140 still has to be filed within 180 days of certification, and a wage determination is still valid for 90 days to a year. Recruitment has to start while it's valid. The proposal also writes into the rule what DOL already does in practice: filing online, electronic signatures, and decisions sent to USCIS directly.

In the proposal, DOL puts the net savings at about $164 million a year, almost all of it from dropping print newspaper ads. The costs it counts are far smaller, among them the longer notice of filing, the layoff notices and full recruitment for Schedule A jobs.

## When it would apply

A final rule would take effect 60 days after it's published, and DOL proposes 30 more days to switch over. A PERM application or wage request filed in the first 90 days after the final rule's publication would be judged under today's rules. Anything filed from day 90 on would follow the new ones. The filing date alone decides which rules apply, so each application answers to one set.

## One timing that reads two ways

The proposal states the filing window twice. Proposed 656.17(d) says the ads end 30 to 60 days before filing. Proposed 656.10(b) says the application is filed within 30 days after the recruitment is completed. DOL's explanation puts both in one sentence: finish recruiting 30 to 60 days before filing, then file 1 to 29 days after the last recruitment activity ends. As written, the two are hard to square, and the text doesn't say whether "completed" means something that comes after the ads end. It's the kind of point a comment can ask DOL to settle.

## What the layoff bar could reach

DOL's own files give a rough sense of scale. Form 9089 asks whether the employer laid anyone off in the job's occupation, or a related one, in the area in the 6 months before filing. In fiscal year 2025 (October 2024 to September 2025), 6,806 of the 146,939 PERM applications DOL decided answered yes, or 4.6%, and 6,408 of those were certified. The proposed bar looks back 2 years, four times as far, so that count is a floor. The two tests aren't worded the same way ("related" on the form, "same or similar" in the proposal), so read it as a guide.

The answers bunch up at a few large employers. Microsoft had the most yes answers in fiscal year 2025: 2,492 of its 3,157 decided applications. Oracle came next with 781 of 1,222. Wipro, Tata Consultancy Services and Capgemini answered yes on almost every application they had decided that year. A yes doesn't mean a bar would apply, because the bar turns on the occupation and the area of each job. It does show where the 2-year look-back would be tested first. Our [layoff disclosures page](/perm-layoff-disclosures) has the answers by year, employer and job.

## What it means for a case today

Nothing changes until a final rule takes effect. A PERM filed now is judged under 20 CFR 656 as it reads today: the last mandatory recruitment step at least 30 days before filing and none more than 180 days before (20 CFR 656.17(e)), the layoff look-back in 656.17(k), and records kept under 656.10(f). A proposal can change before it's final, so planning recruitment around it means planning around text that may move. The attorney on the case is the person to ask how a final rule might touch a specific filing.

## How to comment

Comments go through [regulations.gov](https://www.regulations.gov) under docket ETA-2026-0133. DOL asks that each comment name the section and wording it's about and explain the change it suggests. Everything sent is posted publicly, personal details included, so leave out anything private.

DOL also asks open questions it hasn't settled. The largest is about fully remote jobs: whether they should qualify for PERM at all, need a minimum of on-site work, or face some other limit. It also asks which place should set the prevailing wage for a remote job: the worker's location, the employer's nearest office, the higher of the two, or a national figure.

## A different proposal that's easy to confuse with it

DOL published a separate PERM proposal on March 27, 2026: "Improving Wage Protections for the Temporary and Permanent Employment of Certain Foreign Nationals in the United States" (RIN 1205-AC30, 91 FR 15454). That one would change how the four prevailing wage levels are computed for PERM and for H-1B, H-1B1 and E-3 labor condition applications. It's about wages. RIN 1205-AC29 is about the labor market test.
